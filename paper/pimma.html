Post-selection inference in multiverse meta-analysis

Introduction

Doing research can be hard. Researchers must make many decisions during a study. These decisions concern choosing between many measurement instruments, data pre-processing, and statistical models. Each decision is usually reasonable and defensible but often arbitrary. Together, these decisions are known as researcher degrees of freedom (Simmons et al., 2011).

Researcher degrees of freedom are especially common in psychology and the social sciences, but they also occur in other fields such as medicine and biology (Errington et al., 2021; Nosek et al., 2022). In psychology for example, phenomena are often hard to define and causal relationships between variables are difficult to establish (Eronen & Bringmann, 2021). There are numerous measurement instruments, which are often poorly validated (Flake & Fried, 2020). Data analysis is also complex and rarely guided by a single optimal solution. Together, these features make it difficult to justify one analytic approach over another. In practice, there is often no single best procedure for data collection, pre-processing, or analysis. Researchers must therefore choose one path among many valid ones in the so-called “garden of forking paths” (Gelman & Loken, 2013). Importantly, different choices can lead to different results. Even with the same data and research question, different analytical strategies may yield different conclusions (Silberzahn et al., 2018). Researchers therefore often face a multiverse of equally reasonable outcomes (Steegen et al., 2016).

Because researchers operate within a multiverse of possible choices, analytic decisions often depend on the observed data. Even when researchers do not engage in questionable research practices, such as p-hacking—testing many analytic options until a non-significant result becomes significant—they implicitly face a multiple comparisons problem (Gelman & Loken, 2013). Without proper adjustment, this process inflates the false positive rate beyond the nominal level (e.g., α = 5%). As a result, researcher degrees of freedom increase the prevalence of false positive findings in the literature and are therefore considered one of the causes of the replication crisis (Open Science Collaboration, 2015; Wicherts et al., 2016).

Importantly, researcher degrees of freedom are unavoidable. They are a natural part of empirical research. The problem lies not in their existence but in their opacity. When only one analytic path is reported, reviewers and readers cannot assess how results depend on alternative choices or how robust the findings truly are.

Multiverse Analysis was proposed to address this problem by making the full set of analytic choices and results transparent (Steegen et al., 2016). The core idea is simple: the data are analyzed using all reasonable combinations of analytic choices, and then the full set of results is reported (Simonsohn et al., 2020; Steegen et al., 2016). This approach makes explicit the variability from analytic choices and allows readers to evaluate how conclusions depend on arbitrary decisions.

Although Multiverse Analysis was developed mainly for primary studies, meta-analyses face the same challenges. A Meta-Analysis aims to quantitatively synthesize evidence across studies (Borenstein et al., 2009). However, meta-analyses are also affected by researcher degrees of freedom. Researchers must choose, for example, which studies to include, which effect size metrics to use, and whether to apply fixed-effect or random-effects models. Each decision can be reasonable, yet it can influence the final result. This becomes even more relevant with meta-analyses with complex data structures (e.g., multilevel and/or multivariate) that often require imputing or assuming missing information for effect size computation.

This issue is particularly important because meta-analyses sit at the top of the hierarchy of evidence. They often inform theory, clinical guidelines, and policy decisions. Therefore, if meta-analytic results are sensitive to arbitrary analytic choices, conclusions may be misleading. Assessing robustness is therefore crucial. For this reason, multiverse approaches can be naturally applied to meta-analyses.

Multiverse Meta-Analysis allows researchers to examine how meta-analytical results change across different analytic specifications. This approach is useful for identifying fragile evidence. If conclusions change dramatically after small analytic changes, the results should be interpreted carefully. Such fragility may point to weak theory, poor measurement, or heterogeneous effects. In contrast, results that remain consistent across specifications indicate robust evidence and stronger confidence in the conclusions.

However, despite these advantages, current methods for Multiverse Analysis are limited. Many are purely descriptive, such as the original Multiverse Analysis method proposed by (Steegen et al., 2016) or the Vibration of Effects (Patel et al., 2015), and do not support formal inference. Even existing inferential approaches face constraints. Specification Curve Analysis (SCA) (Simonsohn et al., 2020), for example, is one of the most widely used inferential Multiverse Meta-Analysis tools (see, e.g., (Pietschnig et al., 2022; Plessen et al., 2023)). SCA tests a joint null hypothesis across all specifications, evaluating whether at least one specification differs from the null value. However, this test provides limited insight (Oberauer & Lewandowsky, 2019, p. 1609). This procedure provides only weak control of the Family-Wise Error Rate (FWER) and supports inference exclusively at the level of the joint null hypothesis. Although SCA visually displays the distribution of effect sizes and their statistical significance, the inferential component is limited to this global test and does not extend to individual specifications. In addition, SCA is mainly suited for linear models and does not account for violations of parametric assumptions that are common in random-effects meta-analyses (Follmann & Proschan, 1999). These limitations are especially problematic for meta-analyses, which often guide clinical and policy decisions.

For these reasons, we instead propose using the Post-selection Inference in Multiverse Analysis (PIMA) (Girardi et al., 2024) framework for Multiverse Meta-Analysis. PIMA provides a formal inferential approach that explicitly accounts for multiple comparisons across analytic specifications. It also enables selective inference by allowing researchers to identify even a single significant specification within the entire multiverse while controlling the Type I error rate at a chosen level (e.g., α = 0.05) and maintaining good statistical power. Additionally, by using permutation tests, PIMA does not rely on standard parametric assumptions, which can be unreliable in meta-analyses with a small number of studies or heterogeneous effects (Follmann & Proschan, 1999). Taken together, these features make PIMA particularly appropriate for Multiverse Meta-Analysis. However, to date, PIMA has not been applied in this context.

This paper provides the first such application. First, we describe the statistical principles of PIMA and explain why they are well suited for multiverse meta-analytical settings. Second, we present an empirical application. We apply PIMA to a Multiverse Meta-Analysis of the association between brain size and intelligence (Pietschnig et al., 2022). Finally, we discuss the results and their implications for robustness assessment in meta-analytical research.

Mixed-Effects Meta-Regression

In this section, we present a general formulation of the mixed-effects meta-regression model, following the notation of Viechtbauer et al. (2015). In a meta-analysis with k independent studies, let y_i denote the observed effect size estimate in the ith study, with corresponding sampling variance v_i. The model can be written as

\begin{aligned} \mathbf{y} &= \mathbf{X} \boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{e}, \\ \mathbf{u} &\sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \tau^2\mathbf{I}), \\ \mathbf{e} &\sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{V}), \end{aligned} \tag{1}

where \mathbf{y} is the k \times 1 vector of observed effect size estimates, \mathbf{X} is the k \times (p+1) model matrix, and \boldsymbol{\beta} is the vector of p+1 regression coefficients. The first column of \mathbf{X} contains ones and represents the intercept, while the remaining p columns contain the moderator values. The vector \mathbf{u} contains the study-level random effects and is assumed to follow a normal distribution with mean zero and variance \tau^2, whereas \mathbf{e} contains the within-study sampling errors. The sampling errors are assumed to be normally distributed with diagonal variance-covariance matrix \mathbf{V} = \operatorname{diag}(v_1,\ldots,v_k), where the sampling variances v_i are treated as known. It follows that the marginal distribution of the observed effect size estimates is

\mathbf{y} \sim \mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right), \qquad \boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \mathbf{V} + \tau^2 \mathbf{I}. \tag{2}

For a fixed value of \tau^2, the regression coefficients can be estimated by weighted least squares. Let \mathbf{W} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} = (\mathbf{V} + \tau^2 \mathbf{I})^{-1}. The estimator of \boldsymbol{\beta} is then

\mathbf{b} = (\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{y}. \tag{3}

Since \tau^2 is unknown, the weights used in practice are obtained by replacing \tau^2 with an estimate \hat{\tau}^2, so that \hat{\mathbf{W}} = (\mathbf{V}+\hat{\tau}^2\mathbf{I})^{-1}. Several estimators have been proposed for \tau^2 (see Viechtbauer2005-yh?). We focus here on the DerSimonian–Laird estimator (DerSimonian1986-ju?), as a commonly used non-iterative method-of-moments estimator, and on restricted maximum likelihood, which is the estimator used in the present analyses.

Both estimators can be expressed using the matrix

\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{W} - \mathbf{W}\mathbf{X} (\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{W}. \tag{4}

For the DerSimonian–Laird estimator, \mathbf{W} is computed using only the inverse sampling variances, that is, w_i = v_i^{-1}. The estimator is given by

\hat{\tau}^2_{\mathrm{DL}} = \frac{ \mathbf{y}^{\top}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{y} - (k-p-1) }{ \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{P}) }. \tag{5}

Because \tau^2 is a variance component, negative values of \hat{\tau}^2_{\mathrm{DL}} are truncated to zero.

Likelihood-based estimators are obtained from the marginal normal model in Equation 2. The maximum likelihood log-likelihood is

\ell_{\mathrm{ML}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}, \tau^2) = -\frac{1}{2} \left[ k\log(2\pi) + \log|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}| + (\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta})^{\top} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} (\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}) \right]. \tag{6}

For a fixed value of \tau^2, maximizing Equation 6 with respect to \boldsymbol{\beta} gives the weighted least squares estimator in Equation 3. Substituting this estimator into the likelihood yields the profiled log-likelihood

\ell_{\mathrm{ML}}^{p}(\tau^2) = -\frac{1}{2} \left[ k\log(2\pi) + \log|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}| + \mathbf{y}^{\top}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{y} \right]. \tag{7}

The maximum likelihood estimate of the residual heterogeneity variance is obtained by maximizing the profiled log-likelihood in Equation 7 over the parameter space \tau^2 \geq 0.

However, maximum likelihood estimators of variance components are generally downward biased because they do not account for the loss of degrees of freedom associated with estimating the fixed effects. Restricted maximum likelihood addresses this issue by maximizing a likelihood based on linear combinations of the data that remove the fixed effects. In the present model, the restricted log-likelihood can be written as

\ell_{\mathrm{REML}}(\tau^2) = -\frac{1}{2} \left[ (k-p-1)\log(2\pi) + \log|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}| + \log|\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X}| + \mathbf{y}^{\top}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{y} \right]. \tag{8}

The REML estimate of the residual heterogeneity variance is obtained by maximizing the restricted log-likelihood in Equation 8 over the parameter space \tau^2 \geq 0.

After \hat{\tau}^2_{\mathrm{REML}} has been obtained, the final estimates of the meta-regression coefficients are computed using \tau^2_{\mathrm{REML}} into Equation 3.

Several methods have been proposed to perform statistical inference on the regression coefficients in mixed-effects meta-regression models (see Viechtbauer et al., 2015). Here, we consider the three main approaches that will be compared in the simulation study: Wald-type inference, the Knapp–Hartung method (Knapp2003-qx?), and permutation testing (Follmann & Proschan, 1999; Higgins2004-gf?). The Wald-type and Knapp–Hartung methods are both based on test statistics obtained by dividing the estimated regression coefficient b_j by its corresponding standard error. In the following, b_j denotes the estimate of the coefficient \beta_j, with j = 0,1,\ldots,p when the intercept is included and j = 1,\ldots,p when inference is restricted to the moderator effects.

Under the standard Wald-type approach, the variance-covariance matrix of the estimated regression coefficients is estimated as

\hat{\mathbf{V}}_{\mathbf{b}} = (\mathbf{X}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{W}}\mathbf{X})^{-1}, \tag{9}

where

\hat{\mathbf{W}} = (\mathbf{V}+\hat{\tau}^2\mathbf{I})^{-1}.

The standard error of b_j is given by the square root of the corresponding diagonal element of \hat{\mathbf{V}}_{\mathbf{b}}. The Wald-type test statistic is then defined as

z_j = \frac{b_j}{\sqrt{\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(b_j)}}. \tag{10}

Under the null hypothesis H_0:\beta_j = 0, z_j is compared against the critical value calculated at the \alpha level of a standard normal distribution.

The Knapp–Hartung method uses the same coefficient estimates but replaces the standard variance-covariance matrix (see Equation 9) with an adjusted version as \hat{\mathbf{V}}_{\mathbf{b} \mathrm{KH}} = s^2 \hat{\mathbf{V}}_{\mathbf{b}} with s^2 calculated as

s^2 = \frac{ \mathbf{y}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{y} }{ k-p-1 }. \tag{11}

The test statistic is computed as in Equation 10, using the corresponding adjusted standard error, and is compared against a t distribution with k-p-1 degrees of freedom. A truncated version of the Knapp–Hartung method sets s^2 = 1 whenever s^2 < 1, thereby preventing the adjusted standard error from being smaller than the standard Wald-type standard error.

Permutation testing provides a non-parametric alternative for testing moderator effects. For a given coefficient b_j, the observed Wald-type statistic z_j is first computed from the original data. The rows of the model matrix \mathbf{X} are then permuted, thereby breaking the association between the observed effect sizes and the moderator values while preserving the distribution of the moderators. For each permutation m = 1,\ldots,M, the meta-regression model is refitted and the corresponding test statistic z_j^{(m)} is computed. The permutation distribution of z_j is given by the set of statistics obtained under these permuted data sets. For a two-sided test, the permutation p value can be computed as the proportion of permuted statistics that are at least as extreme as the observed statistic,

p_{\mathrm{perm}} = \frac{ 1 + \sum_{m=1}^{M} \mathbb{I}\left( |z_j^{(m)}| \geq |z_j| \right) }{ 1 + M }. \tag{12}

When all k! permutations are enumerated, this procedure yields the exact permutation test. When k! is too large, the permutation distribution is approximated using a sufficiently large number of random permutations.

Meta-regression using flipscores

The mixed-effects meta-regression model can be reformulated as a score-based test. This provides the link between moderator testing in meta-analysis and the sign-flipping score framework used by PIMA. The key change is that the resampling is no longer applied to the rows of the model matrix, as in the permutation test described above, but to the study-level contributions to the score.

To align the notation with the sign-flipping score literature, we partition the design matrix into focal and nuisance components. In this section, \mathbf{X} denotes the k \times q matrix of focal predictors, whose coefficients are tested, and \mathbf{Z} denotes the k \times r matrix of nuisance predictors. The full design matrix is therefore [\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Z}]. This differs from the previous section, where \mathbf{X} denoted the complete meta-regression design matrix.

The model can be written as

\mathbf{y} \sim \mathcal{N} \left( \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{Z}\boldsymbol{\gamma}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \right), \qquad \boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \mathbf{V} + \tau^2\mathbf{I}. \tag{13}

We test H_0:\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{\beta}_0, usually with \boldsymbol{\beta}_0=\mathbf{0}, while \boldsymbol{\gamma} and \tau^2 remain nuisance parameters. Under the null model, we estimate \hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_0 and \hat{\tau}_0^2 and define

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_0 = \mathbf{V} + \hat{\tau}_0^2\mathbf{I}, \qquad \widehat{\mathbf{W}}_0 = \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_0^{-1}. \tag{14}

The fitted values and residuals under the null are

\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_0 = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}_0 + \mathbf{Z}\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_0, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{r}}_0 = \mathbf{y} - \hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_0. \tag{15}

For fixed \hat{\tau}_0^2, the ordinary score for \boldsymbol{\beta} is k^{-1/2}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_0\hat{\mathbf{r}}_0. However, because the nuisance coefficients are estimated under the null, the ordinary score must be replaced by the effective score (De Santis et al., 2025; Hemerik et al., 2020). Define the weighted hat matrix for the nuisance design as

\mathbf{H}_Z = \widehat{\mathbf{W}}_0^{1/2} \mathbf{Z} \left( \mathbf{Z}^{\top} \widehat{\mathbf{W}}_0 \mathbf{Z} \right)^{-1} \mathbf{Z}^{\top} \widehat{\mathbf{W}}_0^{1/2}. \tag{16}

The effective score, evaluated under H_0, is then

\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{eff}} = k^{-1/2} \mathbf{X}^{\top} \widehat{\mathbf{W}}_0^{1/2} (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}_Z) \widehat{\mathbf{W}}_0^{1/2} \hat{\mathbf{r}}_0. \tag{17}

This expression is the meta-regression analogue of the effective score used in flipscores. It removes from the focal score the component explained by the nuisance score. Equivalently, it can be written as a sum of study-level effective score contributions,

\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{eff}} = k^{-1/2} \sum_{i=1}^{k} \boldsymbol{\nu}_i, \tag{18}

where \boldsymbol{\nu}_i is the contribution of study i to the effective score. With a single focal moderator and an intercept-only nuisance model, this reduces to

\nu_i = (x_i-\bar{x}_w) \hat{w}_{0i} \hat{r}_{0i}, \qquad \bar{x}_w = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\hat{w}_{0i}x_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\hat{w}_{0i}}. \tag{19}

Thus, in the simplest case, the effective score is a weighted association between the centered moderator and the residuals under the null model. Under H_0, the effective score contributions have mean zero and are the quantities to be sign-flipped (De Santis et al., 2025; Girardi et al., 2024; Hemerik et al., 2020).

Let \mathbf{F}^{(b)}=\operatorname{diag}(g_1^{(b)},\ldots,g_k^{(b)}) be a diagonal sign-flipping matrix, with g_i^{(b)}\in\{-1,+1\}. The first flip is fixed as the identity, \mathbf{F}^{(1)}=\mathbf{I}, and the remaining B-1 matrices are sampled at random. For flip b, the flipped effective score is

\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(b)} = k^{-1/2} \mathbf{X}^{\top} \widehat{\mathbf{W}}_0^{1/2} (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}_Z) \widehat{\mathbf{W}}_0^{1/2} \mathbf{F}^{(b)} \hat{\mathbf{r}}_0, \tag{20}

or, equivalently,

\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(b)} = k^{-1/2} \sum_{i=1}^{k} g_i^{(b)} \boldsymbol{\nu}_i. \tag{21}

Following the standardized sign-flip score test, each flipped score is standardized by its flip-specific variance (De Santis et al., 2025; Girardi et al., 2024). In the present meta-regression setting, the variance estimator is

\widehat{\mathbf{C}}^{(b)} = k^{-1} \mathbf{X}^{\top} \widehat{\mathbf{W}}_0^{1/2} (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}_Z) \mathbf{F}^{(b)} (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}_Z) \mathbf{F}^{(b)} (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{H}_Z) \widehat{\mathbf{W}}_0^{1/2} \mathbf{X}. \tag{22}

For a univariate focal parameter, we use

T^{(b)} = \left| \frac{ S_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(b)} }{ \sqrt{\widehat{C}^{(b)}} } \right|. \tag{23}

For a multidimensional focal parameter, we define \widetilde{\mathbf{S}}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(b)}=(\widehat{\mathbf{C}}^{(b)})^{-1/2}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(b)} and use the quadratic statistic

T^{(b)} = \widetilde{\mathbf{S}}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(b)\top} \widetilde{\mathbf{S}}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(b)}. \tag{24}

The sign-flipping p value for a single specification is

p_{\mathrm{flip}} = \frac{ \sum_{b=1}^{B} \mathbb{I} \left( T^{(b)} \geq T^{(1)} \right) }{B}. \tag{25}

This test keeps the null model fixed and constructs the reference distribution by sign-flipping the study-level effective score contributions. It therefore differs from the classical permutation test, which permutes moderator values and refits the model.

This model inherites all the advantage of the flipscores testing framework in terms of rubustaness to variance misspesification. A more pratically relevant advantge is merely computational. While standard permutatation tests requires estimating all focal and nouisance parameters (including \tau^2) the flipscores method requires refitting the model once for each tested coefficient. We explored the computational advantages in the supplementary materials but a meta-analysis with k = 100 studies and 3 predictors using the metafor::permutest() function takes roughly 14 seconds while using the flipscores approach takes 0.3 seconds.

From flipscores to PIMA

The score sign-flipping test provides inference for a single meta-analytic specification. PIMA extends this logic to a multiverse by applying the same sign-flipping transformations across all admissible specifications and using the empirical distribution of the maximum statistic for post-selection inference (Girardi et al., 2024).

Let s=1,\ldots,S index the set of meta-analytic specifications. Specifications may differ in inclusion criteria, effect-size metric, moderator coding, heterogeneity estimator, or model structure. For each specification s, the null model is fitted and the statistic T_s^{(b)} is computed for each flip b=1,\ldots,B, with b=1 denoting the observed data.

The same sign vector is used across specifications. If a specification contains only a subset of studies or effect sizes, the corresponding flip matrix is obtained by restricting the common sign vector to the units included in that specification. This preserves the dependence induced by shared studies, shared effect sizes, and overlapping analytic choices.

For each flip b, define the maximum statistic across the multiverse as

M^{(b)} = \max_{s=1,\ldots,S} T_s^{(b)}. \tag{26}

The global multiverse null hypothesis states that the focal parameter is null in all specifications. Its p value is

p_{\mathrm{global}} = \frac{ \sum_{b=1}^{B} \mathbb{I} \left( M^{(b)} \geq M^{(1)} \right) }{B}. \tag{27}

Specification-wise adjusted p values are obtained by comparing each observed statistic with the same empirical distribution of maxima:

p_{s,\mathrm{adj}} = \frac{ \sum_{b=1}^{B} \mathbb{I} \left( M^{(b)} \geq T_s^{(1)} \right) }{B}. \tag{28}

A specification is selected as significant when p_{s,\mathrm{adj}}\leq\alpha. Since the reference distribution is based on the maximum statistic across all specifications, the procedure controls the family-wise error rate over the multiverse. This yields the meta-analytic analogue of PIMA: within each specification, inference is based on a sign-flipping effective score test; across specifications, post-selection inference is obtained through the max-T distribution.

Simulation study

We performed a simulation study to evaluate the inferential performance of the flipmeta methods compared to the wald-type, knapp and ahartung method and the cluster robust method. We did not evaluated the classical permutation-based method based on permuting the rows of the model matrix given the computational time.

We simulated data following the Viechtbauer et al. (2015) approach, according to the mixed-effects meta-regression models and assuming that the k studies compared an experimental (E) and control groups (C) on a quantitiative outcome. The groups are sampled by two normal distribution with common variance. Then the effect size \theta for the ith study is

\theta_i = \frac{\mu_i^E - \mu_i^C}{\sigma_i},

where the unbiased estimator y_i can be obtained substituting the estimated quantities \bar x_i^E, \bar x_i^C and s_i that is the pooled standard deviation and applying the small sample adjustement as reported in ?@eq-g.

y_i = \left( 1 - \frac{3}{4\left(n_i^E + n_i^C\right) - 9}. \right)d_i Where d_i = (\bar x_i^E - \bar x_i^C) / s_i. The sampling variance v_i can be calculated with

v_i = \frac{1}{n_i^E} + \frac{1}{n_i^C} + \frac{y_i^2}{2 (n_i^E + n_i^C)}. The true effects \theta_i are expressed as

\boldsymbol{\theta} = \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{Z}\mathbf{\gamma}, where \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} is the model matrix and coefficients vector for the focal predictors, \mathbf{Z}\mathbf{\gamma} is the model matrix and coefficients vector of the nuiscance covariates and \mathbf{u} is the vector of random effects. Under the null hypothesis H_0, \mathbf{\beta} = 0 and under the alterative hypothesis H_1 each \beta = [0.1, 0.3, 0.5] respectively as small, medium and large effects. Under both hypotheses, each \gamma = [0.1, 0.3, 0.5]. Focal and nuiscance predictors are sampled from a multivariate normal distribution with unit variance and compound symmetry structure with correlation \rho = 0.5. The random effects \mathbf{u} were simulated from a normal distribution with \tau^2 = [0, 0.01, 0.1, 0.5] covering the equal-effects case when \tau^2 = 0 and small to large absolute heterogeneity considering the scale of the effect. We simulate meta-analyses with k = 10, 20, 40 and 100 studies. Finally we simulated sample sizes for each study from a negative binomial distribution setting the mean to N = 10, 30, 50, 80 where N = n^E = n^C and the dispersion parameter as N/3. In this way the variance is twice the mean creating a more realistic heterogeneous sample sizes across the k studies.

For each of the scenario we simulated 10,000 meta-analyses, fitting the statistical models using metafor (metafor?) and extracting the p values calculated with the Wald, Knapp, robust variance estimator and using the flipmeta approach. We fitted both the equal-effects (ignoring \tau^2) and the random-effects model for each scenario. For the flipmeta approach we performed B = 5000 sign flips in each condition. The number of possible permutations is 2^k then when 2^k < B we run all the possible permutations obtaining an exact test with a minimum p value of 1/2^k. For each scenario we calculated the proportion of rejected null hypotheses estimating the type-1 error rate under H_0 and the statistical power under H_1.
